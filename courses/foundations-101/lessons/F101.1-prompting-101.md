# F101.1 — Prompting 101: Match the Prompt to the Work

**Duration:** 3 minutes 24 seconds (published cut; script estimate was 3:40)  
**Timing basis:** 135 spoken words per minute  
**Audience assumption:** The learner has used AI for a simple question or summary  
**Key concept:** Modern models understand routine requests with less instruction, but complex work still needs a clear brief and human steering.  
**Learner outcome:** Start a multi-step analytics task with a clear brief, then review and steer the work until it is defensible.

## A-roll script

(0:00–0:07) | Series Introduction

Welcome to the Analytics AI Enablement education series. Today in Foundations, we focus on prompting.

(0:07–0:43) | Why Shorter Prompts Work Today

Prompting is easier than it was two years ago. Frontier AI models are smarter, and after initial training, they are further refined—or post-trained—for agentic work.

A person describes a task; the AI uses files, tools, or MCP connections; returns a result; receives feedback; and continues.

For an analytics team, think of a new analyst who has already practiced many briefs and review cycles. The model already understands much of this work pattern, so routine tasks often need only a direct request.

(0:43–1:11) | Keep Simple Work Simple

Suppose you have AI-generated meeting notes. You can ask: “What did I promise my manager or stakeholders I would deliver next week?” For a straightforward summary, that may be all you need.

You still own the answer. Check the notes before treating the response as fact. But do not turn a simple request into an essay just because an AI is doing it.

(1:11–2:02) | Smarter Does Not Mean Mind Reader

The challenge begins when a task has multiple reasonable interpretations.

Imagine asking an enterprise search agent, “Who was in charge of Project X?” It might find the person who ran operations, even though you wanted the person who set strategy or made the final decision.

A better prompt would say: “We are now in 2026. For the 2025 cycle of Project X, who presented our team’s predictions to stakeholders? Who ultimately decided when to implement them? Use the latest documentation and cite the evidence.”

The improvement is not fancy wording. It is clearer delegation. AI is like a junior colleague: it works better when you define the responsibility, time period, evidence, and answer you need.

(2:02–3:03) | Brief, Review, and Steer

For work involving SQL, data preparation, analysis, visualization, and a recommendation, begin with a five-part brief.

**Goal:** What decision or result should the work support?

**Data & tools:** Which datasets, files, tools, or approved MCP connections should the AI use?

**Context:** Which metric definitions, business rules, constraints, and assumptions matter? What uncertainty should make the AI stop and ask you?

**Output:** What deliverable do you need: SQL, an analysis, a visualization, or an HTML report?

**Manual verification:** Which known totals, expected ranges, source records, or tests should the AI use—and which results will you verify yourself?

AI can generate the analysis, visualization, or HTML, but you still own the work. “The AI generated it” does not transfer accountability.

Before presenting to stakeholders, understand the data, assumptions, calculations, and checks well enough to explain how the result was produced.

(3:03–3:30) | Your Judgment Steers the Work

Your judgment supplies the taste and business context. You know whether a pie chart will frustrate the stakeholder, which assumptions need another scenario, and when the evidence is strong enough to use.

Keep routine prompts short. For complex work, start with a clear brief, review what the AI produces, and steer it until you can explain and defend the result.

(3:30–3:39) | Continue Learning

Explore other videos in this series, and leave a comment in the Analytics AI knowledge sharing channel so we can improve the course.

## On-screen cues

### Visual system

- Keep the presenter on the left and the teaching canvas on the right. Use the organic split edge from the Foundations 101 frame.
- Use a white canvas, dark-ink text, Literata headings, and IBM Plex Sans body text. Do not add subtitles, a persistent logo, or decorative B-roll.
- Use blue for structure, pale blue for supporting information, orange for human review, and dark ink for business context.
- Keep the five prompt labels visually stable throughout the lesson. Use both a written label and a distinct blob treatment so meaning never depends on color alone.

| Prompt part | Stable treatment |
|---|---|
| **Goal** | Solid blue circular blob with white text |
| **Data & tools** | Pale-blue wide capsule with a blue border and blue text |
| **Context** | Dark-ink rectangular blob with white text |
| **Output** | White document-shaped blob with a dark-ink border, blue text, and a blue file icon |
| **Manual verification** | Solid orange check-shaped blob with dark-ink text |

- Arrows must explain a relationship or workflow. Draw them between steps as the narration introduces the connection; do not use arrows as decoration.
- Reveal the most important element first, then supporting details. Let every completed visual hold long enough to be read before it changes.
- Use deliberate motion verbs: arrows **draw**, prompt cards **morph**, labeled rows **stack**, and verification marks **check**. Avoid repeating the same fade-in for every element.

### Example prompt shown on screen

Build this prompt as one compact, asymmetrical card. Place each label in its colored blob and its instruction in a connected row.

> **Goal:** Explain why weekly active customers fell in the latest complete week versus the prior four-week average.  
> **Data & tools:** Analyze `weekly-customers.csv`, use `metric-definitions.md` for definitions, and use the approved read-only warehouse connection to reconcile totals.  
> **Context:** Use the documented metric definition, separate facts from hypotheses, and stop if missing data or conflicting definitions could change the result.  
> **Output:** Create a one-page HTML report with a bar chart, an evidence table, and three findings—not a 2,000-word document.  
> **Manual verification:** Have the AI reconcile the latest-week total and show its formulas and sources; then spot-check three records yourself.

## Immediate exercise and feedback loop

Choose one real analytics task with at least two steps. Write one line for each part: Goal, Data & tools, Context, Output, and Manual verification.

Run the prompt and inspect the first result. Correct one assumption or ambiguity, ask the AI to continue, then perform the manual verification you defined.

## Boundary

A clear prompt cannot create missing access, data, permission, domain knowledge, or evidence. It should make those gaps visible before the agent turns an unsupported assumption into a polished answer.
