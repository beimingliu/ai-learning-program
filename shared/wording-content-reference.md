# Wording and Content Reference

**Purpose:** Canonical voice guide for writing and revising the AI Learning Program's video scripts. Use this during the later tone pass across all 19 lessons. Preserve each lesson's learning outcome, exercise, boundary, and track placement unless a separate content change is approved.

**Reference version:** 1.2  
**Last reviewed:** August 19, 2026  
**Primary model lesson:** [F101.0 — From Chatbot to Agent](../courses/foundations-101/lessons/F101.0-from-chatbot-to-agent.md)

## Tone north star

Make the learner feel:

1. **Seen:** The script names a recognizable work experience.
2. **Relieved:** The difficulty is understandable, not evidence that the learner is incapable or late.
3. **Productively unsettled:** The current behavior has a specific cost worth changing.
4. **Capable:** One bounded action is available now.

The desired voice is calm, candid, practical, and slightly challenging. It should sound like a thoughtful colleague who respects the learner's judgment—not a futurist, productivity influencer, compliance narrator, or technical gatekeeper.

## Productive tension, not fear

The course may describe the cost of remaining in chatbot-only or unverified work. It should not use catastrophe, status anxiety, or job-loss threats as motivation.

A useful tension sequence is:

> **Recognizable behavior → understandable reason → hidden cost → achievable shift**

Example:

> Many people ask AI a question, receive an answer, and close the window. That makes sense: chat was the first behavior these tools taught. The limitation is that one-off answers disappear while verified workflows become easier to repeat. The next step is not learning every AI feature; it is delegating one bounded job.

### Tension must be

- **Specific:** Name the actual cost, such as rework, lost context, weak evidence, or a workflow that never becomes reusable.
- **Proportional:** Do not turn a low-risk learning gap into a crisis.
- **Brief:** Move from tension to explanation and action quickly.
- **Resolvable:** The lesson must provide a realistic next move within its duration.

### Do not motivate with

- Predictions that the learner will lose a job or become irrelevant.
- Claims that “everyone else” is already ahead.
- Rankings, status comparisons, or attention-span praise.
- Ridicule of chatbot use, nontechnical work, cautious users, or setup difficulty.
- Unsupported claims that AI is inevitable, transformative, superhuman, or universally faster.

## Audience stance

Assume the learner is competent in their own work and unfamiliar with this operating model.

- Describe a behavior before judging an outcome.
- Normalize reasonable causes: the interface taught chat, policy may be unclear, access may be missing, or the first task may have been poorly chosen.
- Use **we** when naming a common trap. Use **you** for a concrete action the learner can take.
- Include the instructor in the learning curve when appropriate; do not speak from a pedestal.
- Treat caution as useful information. Convert it into a bounded first step instead of telling the learner not to worry.
- Never equate terminal fluency, prompt volume, or model knowledge with intelligence or seriousness.

## The program's core narrative

The program is not selling “more AI.” It is teaching a progression:

```text
Chatbot answer
    ↓
Bounded agent task
    ↓
Verified artifact
    ↓
Reusable workflow or better work design
    ↓
Second verified use
```

The recurring promise is:

> One useful task, one independently verified result, then a second win.

Avoid expanding that promise into mastery of every model, tool, connector, or automation pattern.

## Positioning chatbot and agent use

Chatbot use is not wrong. It remains appropriate for questions, exploration, explanation, and quick drafting. Agent use becomes valuable when the learner needs a system to inspect sources, use tools, produce an artifact, observe results, and continue toward a checkable finish line.

### Preferred distinction

- **Chatbot:** “Answer this question.”
- **Agent:** “Complete this bounded job using these sources, constraints, outputs, and checks.”

### Required qualifiers

- An agent can act and iterate; it is not automatically correct.
- Agency is not the same as autonomy. A task can be agentic while remaining read-only or requiring approval.
- The human remains part of the loop through direction, review, verification, interruption, and decision ownership.
- Use **approved agentic surface** when product access or organizational policy matters.

### Preferred agent language

- Delegate a bounded job.
- Give the agent a finish line.
- Name the source, boundary, artifact, and check.
- Review the plan before action.
- Keep the human in the loop.
- Require evidence before use.
- Start read-only and reversible.

### Avoid

- Unleash the agent.
- Put AI on autopilot.
- Let AI do your job.
- AI employee, digital worker, or teammate as a literal claim.
- Set it and forget it.
- Fully autonomous unless that behavior is both accurate and necessary to the lesson.
- Trust the AI.

## Standard emotional and instructional arc

Every 1–3 minute script should use the established five beats, with a tone purpose attached to each one.

### Title-first rule for Foundations

In Foundations 101, the first sentence must state the lesson topic and practical shift. Do not delay the concept with a general trend, extended anxiety hook, or story.

The series opening carries this. Use the fixed pattern, with a comma and a spoken clause — never a colon, which gives the voice nothing to pause on:

> Welcome to the Analytics AI Enablement education series. Today in Foundations, we focus on prompting.

For the 201 tracks, substitute the course name and convert an imperative title to a gerund: *Today in AI Thinking, we focus on diagnosing failure before escalating.*

Preferred:

> In this lesson, we focus on prompting AI agents better. A useful prompt is a clear task brief, not a magic phrase.

Avoid:

> AI is changing quickly, and many people feel overwhelmed by the number of things they need to learn.

Empathy can follow the title-aligned opening when it helps explain a real friction. It should not make the learner wait to discover what the lesson teaches.

### 1. Empathy hook — “I recognize this”

Name one real friction, misconception, or hesitation. Prefer a moment the learner can picture over an abstract trend.

Good:

> You open the AI window, see a blinking cursor, and cannot think of a useful question.

Weak:

> Artificial intelligence is changing the world faster than ever.

### 2. One-sentence concept — “The shift is understandable”

State the durable idea in plain language. A counterintuitive reframe is useful when it resolves the opening tension.

> A prompt is not a magic phrase; it is a task brief for a capable system that does not share the facts in your head.

### 3. Demonstration — “I can see the mechanism”

Show a source, an agent action, and evidence. Use one concrete example rather than a feature list. Make the difference visible before explaining every term.

### 4. Boundary — “I know when this stops applying”

Name one limitation, risk, or false inference. Boundaries should increase confidence through precision, not abruptly reverse the lesson's promise.

> Read-only reduces modification risk; it does not prove the interpretation or authorize the data.

### 5. Immediate action — “I can try this now”

End with one observable action and a tight feedback loop. Do not end on inspiration alone.

> Ask for the plan only. Check whether it names the correct source, deliverable, boundary, and evidence.

## Script pacing

For a typical 2–3 minute, 200–300 word lesson:

- **Opening tension:** 30–55 words.
- **Reframe and concept:** 35–60 words.
- **Mechanism or demonstration:** 80–120 words.
- **Boundary:** 20–40 words.
- **Action and feedback:** 30–55 words.

These are editing ranges, not quotas. One idea and a clear learner action matter more than symmetry.

## Narration mechanics for TTS

Narration is synthesized, not read by a person. Punctuation is the only prosody control, and anything written for the eye rather than the ear degrades the take.

- **No unspaced em-dashes.** `system prompt—the standing instructions` runs the words together. Use a comma, a colon, or a full stop, chosen for the grammatical role. Spacing the dash does not reliably fix it.
- **No colons inside a spoken sentence** where a pause is intended. A colon is silent; a comma or full stop is not.
- **Keep file paths, filenames, and slash commands out of spoken lines.** `~/.claude/CLAUDE.md` is read character by character. Say "your user file" and let the path appear on screen. Same for `/memory`, `/context`, and any `*.html` artifact name.
- **No symbols the voice will name.** `#analytics-ai-knowledge-sharing` is spoken as "number analytics dash…". Write channel and handle names as plain words — "the Analytics AI knowledge sharing channel" — and keep the `#` form in the on-screen cue.
- **Capitalise initialisms in narration.** Lowercase "ai" is read as a word; "AI" is read as the letters.
- **Spell out numbers that carry meaning** — "roughly eighty percent", not "~80%".
- Anything the voice should not say belongs in the on-screen cues, not in the A-roll.

## Track-specific voice

### AI Foundations 101

- Welcoming, concrete, and activation-first.
- Introduce the minimum vocabulary after a useful action is visible.
- Reduce setup shame and prompting mythology.
- Prefer familiar workplace artifacts: CSV, document, summary, comparison, checklist.
- Keep the first write action separate from the source and require a simple check.

### Building with AI 201

- Operational and precise without becoming a command manual.
- Name the visible control, the decision it supports, and the evidence to inspect afterward.
- Use current product labels only when verified and date-stamped.
- Distinguish access, permission, correctness, recovery, and governance.
- Emphasize inspection, reversibility, ownership, and last-tested dates.

### AI Thinking 201

- Vendor-neutral, reflective, and decision-oriented.
- Make abstract judgment concrete through a case, decision owner, evidence source, or stop condition.
- Avoid management slogans and vague calls to “think strategically.”
- Keep AI leverage and human accountability in the same example.
- State uncertainty without using it as an excuse for inaction.

## Canonical vocabulary

Follow [GLOSSARY.md](../GLOSSARY.md). In particular:

- The **model** reasons; **tools** act; **context** informs the current step.
- The **agentic loop** gathers context, acts, observes, verifies, then repeats or stops.
- An **artifact** is a durable output, not merely an answer in the conversation.
- **Verification** connects an output to independent evidence.
- A **human decision owner** remains accountable for consequential judgment or action.
- **Memory** is persisted guidance, not guaranteed truth or enforcement.

Use the five-part task brief consistently:

> **Goal / Data & tools / Context / Output / Manual verification**

Do not introduce competing prompt frameworks in foundational or 101 lessons.

## Wording swaps

| Avoid | Prefer |
|---|---|
| Stop using AI like a chatbot. | Chat is a useful starting point. The next step is delegating a bounded job. |
| Use status comparisons to pressure learners. | State relevant, sourced differences plainly, then give the learner a concrete, nonjudgmental next move. |
| AI can do your work for you. | AI can inspect, retrieve, draft, transform, recommend, or execute selected steps. |
| Just give it more context. | Name the relevant source, rule, example, or definition. |
| Do not be afraid of AI. | Begin with approved, read-only, reversible work. |
| You do not need to be technical. | Use the approved surface that makes the source, action, and evidence easiest to review. |
| Trust, but verify. | Require independent evidence before using the result. |
| The AI knows your project. | The agent can reach the workspace and reasons from the context it has loaded. |
| The agent safely handles it. | The task uses bounded access, conservative permissions, independent checks, and a recovery path. |
| Simply click… / Just run… | Open… / Run… / Choose… followed by the necessary context. |
| This will save hours. | Record the actual time and review work for this task. |
| Perfect prompt. | Clear task brief. |

## Sentence-level style

- Prefer short, complete sentences for spoken clarity.
- Use active verbs: inspect, retrieve, compare, draft, transform, verify, stop, decide.
- Put the important contrast in parallel language: **answer a question / complete a job**.
- Define jargon at first use, then use the canonical term consistently.
- Use contractions when they sound natural aloud.
- Address one objection directly rather than listing every possible concern.
- Use rhetorical questions only when the likely answer is respectful and useful.
- Avoid stacked metaphors. One clear analogy is enough.
- Avoid filler transitions such as “as we all know,” “obviously,” and “it goes without saying.”
- Avoid absolutes including always, never, everyone, no one, and guaranteed unless they describe an enforced rule.

## Content guardrails

- One primary concept and one observable learner action per lesson.
- One demonstration or concrete case.
- One explicit boundary.
- Product claims must be verified against current official documentation and the approved environment.
- Do not invent organization policy, connectors, model access, or permission labels.
- Keep edits local and sources unchanged in early demonstrations.
- A polished result is not evidence.
- A prompt instruction is not a hard control.
- A checkpoint is not universal undo.
- A stronger model does not repair missing access, context, or success criteria.
- Human review must name a real check or decision; “a human is involved” is not enough.

## Later tone-pass workflow

For each existing lesson:

1. Preserve the lesson code, key concept, learner outcome, exercise, boundary, and bridge.
2. Identify shame, hype, feature dumping, abstraction, or unsupported urgency in the current script.
3. Rewrite the hook using a specific workplace moment.
4. Add relief: explain why the current behavior is understandable.
5. State one counterintuitive or clarifying shift.
6. Make the demonstration show source, action, and evidence.
7. Tighten the boundary so it distinguishes the relevant control type.
8. End with one action the learner can perform and check immediately.
9. Read the script aloud; target 200–300 spoken words without compressing clarity.
10. Recheck product claims, links, source mapping, and cross-track independence.

Do not renumber lessons, move topics between tracks, or change capstone requirements during a tone-only pass.

## Tone QA checklist

Before approving a revised script, answer yes to each question:

- Does the opening describe a real learner experience rather than a market trend?
- Does it create useful tension without shame, catastrophe, or status comparison?
- Does the script explain why the learner's current behavior is reasonable?
- Is the lesson's conceptual shift clear in one sentence?
- Does the example show a source, action, artifact or result, and check where relevant?
- Is chatbot use treated as useful but limited rather than foolish?
- Is agent use described as bounded action and iteration rather than magical autonomy?
- Does the boundary state what the technique does not guarantee?
- Is human ownership explicit for consequential judgment or action?
- Can the learner perform the final action immediately and observe whether it worked?
- Are all product-specific statements verified and dated?
- Does the script remain independent of the other 201 track?
